# Chapter 10 — The fundamental frequency generator (O'Shaughnessy F0 algorithm)

Source: Allen, Hunnicutt & Klatt, *From Text to Speech: The MITalk System* (1987), pp. 100–107.

> **Format note:** This is a technical restatement for reimplementation, **not a verbatim transcription**. All numbers, Hz values, percentages, table entries and section numbering are exactly as printed. The prose is paraphrased.

<!-- book p.100 -->

## 10.1 Overview

- F0 helps signal syntactic structure, emphasis and sentence type, together with duration and amplitude.
- The F0 algorithm uses information from both the syntactic and phonological components:
  - **Parser phrase structure** sets the declination lines, the excursion from the declination line through each phrase, and where continuation rises go.
  - **Lexical stress marks and syllable division** set where F0 peaks fall.
  - **Part of speech** sets the relative height of peaks.
  - **Phonemic data** supply segmental influences on F0. These give active variation in peaks and valleys (O'Shaughnessy, 1976).
- **Current output form:** two F0 "target values" per phonetic segment, one for the **onset** and one as a **mid-value**. This adapts the original O'Shaughnessy algorithm, which produced one value every **5 msec**. Targets let F0 be interpolated like the other parameters, at a lower data rate (one or two values per segment instead of one per 5 msec).
- The rises and falls computed for each segment set the targets: the **peak point**, at either the **left or right boundary of stressed vowels in content words**, and the **midpoint** target for other segments. Other midpoint values come from interpolation.
- Inputs: syntactic information from **PARSER** (Ch. 4) and phonemic information from **PROSOD** (Ch. 9), as a PROSOD output file. Output: an **augmented PROSOD file** with the two target values for each segment.

## 10.2 Input

- PARSER output supplies phrase-group information and each word's part of speech.
- Phrase groups recognized: **noun phrases, prepositional phrases, verb phrases, verbal groups**.
- Parts of speech are grouped into the classes of Table 10-1, listed in order of how much they can affect the contour. Entries **in parentheses** are assigned by the F0 algorithm itself and are not used directly in the lexicon. Example: a reflexive pronoun is stored in the lexicon as part of speech PRONOUN with feature REFLEXIVE, but reaches the F0 algorithm simply as PRONOUN.

### Table 10-1: Relative peak levels of words according to their parts of speech

| Level | Part of speech |
|---|---|
| 0 | article |
| 1 | conjunction, relative pronoun |
| 2 | preposition, auxiliary verb, (unstressable modal, vocative) |
| 3 | personal pronoun |
| 6 | verb, demonstrative pronoun |
| 7 | noun, adjective, adverb, contraction |
| 8 | (reflexive pronoun) |
| 9 | stressable modal |
| 10 | quantifier |
| 11 | interrogative adjectives |
| 12 | (negative element) |
| 14 | (sentential adverb) |

- The book says **nine** levels are actually distinguished from one another (the table lists 12 rows).
- Classes from **VERB (Level 6)** upward are important enough to produce a contour peak. Words with these parts of speech are called **"content" words**. Peak height depends on this ordering.
- PHONO1 also labels words "content" or "function". Every PHONO1 "content" word is also a content word here.

<!-- book p.102 -->

- Some parts of speech labelled "function" in PHONO1 are **promoted to content** in the F0 algorithm:
  - Demonstrative pronouns (this, those)
  - Contractions (we'll, boys'll)
  - Modals (should, might, will, can)
  - Quantifiers (several, many)
  - Interrogative adjectives (which, whose)
- The algorithm also needs, for each word: the **number of syllables**, the **location of the stressed syllable**, and **syllable boundaries**. All come from the PROSOD output file. The phonemes in that file give each syllable a structure: an allowed ordering of voiced or unvoiced obstruents, sonorants, and **a single vowel**.

## 10.3 Output

Two possible output files:

1. **(Former method)** A stream of F0 values, one per **5 msec** of the utterance. It can be merged with PHONET output (Ch. 11), which gives the **20** variable parameters every 5 msec. Values come from the F0 change over a syllable, using the syllable's segment durations to make a contour of **constant slope (absolute value)**.
2. **(Current method)** Compute rises and falls on each segment (an intermediate stage of method 1) and use them to set F0 targets for the **midpoint of each segment** and for the **peak point at the left or right boundary of stressed vowels in content words**.
   - A segment's unspecified **onset** value is found by **linear interpolation** between its midpoint target and the midpoint target of the **preceding** segment.
   - F0 can then be computed every 5 msec with the same linear smoothing procedure used for some other parameters, slightly modified to accept the possible extra target value.

Peak placement:

- **Most peaks** go on the **right boundary** of the stressed vowel in a content word. The fall (and any continuation rise) after the peak-forming rise is assigned to the **midpoint or right boundary of the following segment**. It absorbs any fall or rise previously assigned to that segment.
- A peak goes on the **left boundary** of a **"nuclear-stressed"** syllable: the stressed syllable in the **final content word of a phrase that precedes a silence**. Any earlier unassigned rises or falls are absorbed when the peak is assigned.

## 10.4 The O'Shaughnessy fundamental frequency algorithm

A cascade of two systems: the **High Level System** uses syntax to sketch the contour, and the **Low Level System** uses High Level output plus phonemic data to fill in detail.

### 10.4.1 High Level System

Predicts a superposed F0 contour from **sentence type, clause contour, phrase contour, and individual word contour**. The Low Level System then adds segmental effects.

### 10.4.2 Sentence type

Two global **tunes**, chosen by sentence type:

- **Tune A** (mainly declaratives): a **linear falling** F0 trend over its clause, with a **sharp fall** on the **last content word** of the clause and on the words after it.
- **Tune B** (yes/no questions): a **rise**, then a **relatively flat** trend, then a **sharp terminal rise**.

### 10.4.3 Clause contour

A sharp rise at the start of a syntactic unit and a sharp fall at its end. In practice there is only **one** such contour per sentence, because the parser does not identify clauses. It coincides with the tune contour.

### 10.4.4 Phrase contour

In phrases with **two or more content words**: an initial F0 rise begins at the **first** content word, and a final F0 fall begins on the **last** content word. If the phrase is **nonfinal**, a **continuation rise** goes on the **last syllable of the last word**.

### 10.4.5 Word contour

- Content words within a phrase get the most F0 movement. Besides the sharp rise and fall on the first and last content words of a phrase, a **rise-fall** contour is placed on the **stressed syllable of each content word**.
- Rationale (paraphrased): content words are less predictable and carry more information, so they are emphasized. Function words are frequent and their syntax is easy to recognize.

<!-- book p.104 -->

- The amount of F0 movement on a word depends on its **rank in Table 10-1** and its **number of syllables**. Higher rank gives larger excursion.
- **Function words and unstressed syllables of content words get a slight (5 Hz) excursion.**

### 10.4.6 Prosodic indicators

The High Level System passes these to the Low Level System, per word unless noted:

- **accent**: relative importance of the word. It ranges from **"0"** for a one-syllable article to **"11+n"** for a sentential adverb of n syllables.
- **position**: an integer for the word's position in a phrase and the importance of that phrase. Words at punctuation boundaries and at boundaries of large or major phrases get **higher absolute values**.
- **amount of continuation rise**: usually **"0"**. Words ending a **nonfinal phrase** usually get a value reflecting the importance of the syntactic boundary immediately after the word.
- **level**: applies to words in **noun phrases that contain no conjunctions**. It means either that F0 should rise or that F0 should drop on that word. Other words get level **"0"**, meaning a mid-phrase word.
- **tune**: defined on every word. It is **nonzero on the word that ends a clause**.
- **number of phrases**: also passed to the next level.

(The book does not give numeric encodings for position, level, or continuation amount on these pages.)

### 10.4.7 The Low Level System

- Models the effects of phonemics, lexical stress, and syllable counts.
- Syllable count helps set the **height of peaks on lexically stressed syllables**.
- The **first and highest peak** in a sentence is limited to a **maximum of about 190 Hz**. Longer sentences (more syllables) start with higher peaks, which leaves room for later excursions.
- Two lexically stressed syllables separated by unstressed syllables also get **higher peaks**. Their height depends on the number of unstressed syllables in between.
- Phonemic effects: **unvoiced consonants at the start of a stressed syllable** make the contour **fall** rather than rise into the stressed vowel. The rise is added to the vowel's peak instead. See Figure 10-1.
- Order of operations:
  1. Set the **peaks** on lexically stressed syllables.

<!-- book p.105 -->

  2. Assign **falls and rises** around the peaks.
  3. Add **continuation rises** to the last syllable of most non-sentence-final phrases (Figure 10-1). Give sentence-final words rises or falls according to their **tune**.
  4. Finally, set the amount of **fall on the other nonstressed syllables**.

### Figure 10-1: Example of F0 contours

Description (schematic; no numeric axis values are printed):

- Axes: vertical **F0**, horizontal **Time**.
- Column labels across the top, left to right: **V** "stressed", **C**, **C** "unvoiced", **V** "stressed / nucleus". Dotted vertical lines mark segment boundaries.
- A dotted, gently **falling straight line** across the figure is labelled **"Declination line"** (lower right).
- The contour, left to right:
  1. It starts above the declination line and falls steeply into the first boundary area, where a **deep narrow V-shaped dip** reaches almost to the time axis just before the first stressed vowel. This dip is labelled **"Glottal stop"** (glottalization dip, see 10.5).
  2. Out of the dip, through the first stressed V, a **solid** line rises to a **peak at the right boundary of the stressed vowel**. A **dashed** alternative rises to a higher peak at the same place.
  3. The contour **falls linearly** across the C segments to a **valley at the start of the second stressed V**, where the boundary after the "unvoiced" C is. This shows the fall into a stressed syllable that begins with an unvoiced consonant.
  4. In the "stressed nucleus" V, the solid line rises again to a **peak at the left boundary of the nucleus** region (the boundary between "unvoiced C" and "nucleus" columns), consistent with left-boundary peaks on nuclear-stressed syllables. A dashed alternative rises higher here as well.
  5. It **falls** steeply to a low point slightly above/near the declination line, then **rises** at the end. This rise is labelled **"Continuation rise"**.
- Solid and dashed contours: dashed segments show the alternative/higher-peak versions (for example with the extra rise from an unvoiced initial consonant added to the peak). The figure gives no legend.

### Peak heights and declination

- A stressed syllable's peak is **proportional to the accent number** and also **decreases through the sentence**. Peaks lie along a **falling declination line**, so equal-accent peaks get lower from sentence start to end.
- Declination is **steeper for Tune A** and **less steep for Tune B**.

### Rise and fall amounts

- Each content word gets a **rise and fall around the peak of its primary-stressed syllable**.
- **Basic rise = 40 percent** of the distance from the **initial value of the lower declination line** (**110 Hz for Tune A, 125 Hz for Tune B**) to the peak value.
- Boundary-position peaks change this: a **phrase-initial** peak gets **more rise** (a lower valley), and **phrase-final** peaks get **less rise** (a higher valley). Intervening unaccented syllables call for **more rise** on the peaks on either side of them.
- **Basic fall = 20 percent** of the distance from the lower declination line to the peak value. It is **increased for a phrase-final fall**.
- Rises and falls **within a phrase** are reduced a further **30 percent**.

(The amounts of the phrase-initial, phrase-final and phrase-final-fall adjustments are not given numerically on these pages.)

### Accented/unaccented patterns

- The norm is alternating accented and unaccented syllables. **Accented** means the stressed syllable of a content word. **Unaccented** means every other syllable.
- **Adjacent accented syllables:** their rise values are **reduced by 40 percent**.
- **Accented syllables separated by 2, 3, or 4 unaccented syllables:** rise values **increased by 15, 20, and 30 percent** respectively. More unaccented syllables have **no further effect**.
- **Peak height** on an accented syllable **preceded** by **2 or 3** unaccented syllables: **decreased by 15 and 25 percent** respectively.
- **Peak height** on an accented syllable **followed** by **2 or 3** unaccented syllables: **increased by 10 and 15 percent** respectively.
- **Three accented syllables in succession:** F0 on the **second** falls from the first's peak and rises into the third's peak. In other words, its fall and rise swap places in time.
- A word **not covered by a node** and preceded by **three or more** unaccented syllables gets **rise = (its peak value − 95 Hz)**.

### Terminal positions

- **Statement (Tune A):** the last syllable gets a fall value that brings F0 to **75 Hz**. (See 10.5: the final statement value was later lowered by 10 Hz.)
- **Yes/no question (Tune B):** a rise is assigned after the last accented syllable's fall (no rise if that syllable is the last syllable). It gives a **final F0 20 percent higher than any previous peak**.

### Continuation rises

- **16 Hz** (the highest): on the **last syllable of a word** that is followed by a **nonterminal punctuation mark or a conjunction**, provided there has been **no punctuation or conjunction since the last content word**.
- **8 Hz**: on the **last syllable of the last word in a nonfinal phrase**, if **more than five words** have passed since the last word that received a continuation rise.

### Contour between two accented syllables separated by unaccented ones

The connecting contour is either **straight or falling**. Let the difference be (endpoint of the earlier accented syllable) compared with (endpoint of the later one); the text calls it "the difference between the endpoints of the two accented syllables":

- **Difference positive:** adjust the previous fall and the next rise by the same amount, **half the difference**, so F0 stays **flat** across the intermediate unaccented syllables.
- **Difference negative:** spread the fall over the intermediate unaccented syllables in one of two ways:
  - Unaccented syllables **within one phrase**: a **linear** fall. Each successive unaccented syllable gets an **equal share**.
  - Unaccented syllables **not in the same phrase**: a **more exponential** fall, with **earlier** unaccented syllables getting **more** of it. (No exact formula is given.)
- Unaccented syllables that **end a Tune A or Tune B clause** fall or rise in **equal amounts** to the final value.

(The sign convention for "difference" is not defined further in the text.)

## 10.5 Adjustments to the O'Shaughnessy algorithm (as implemented in MITalk)

1. **Third tune for wh-questions** (questions with a question word such as "how" or "who", expecting an answer other than yes/no). It produces a **high peak on the question word**, a **steeper F0 fall than declaratives**, and a **higher peak on the last accented syllable** than declaratives get. (No numbers given.)
2. The **additional 20 percent rise** given to an **initial unvoiced consonant** is added to the **left boundary of the following vowel** instead of to its peak. The contour then falls from the start of the vowel if the peak is at the left boundary.
3. A local **5 Hz perturbation** is inserted on **flat and falling unstressed syllables**, about their **midpoint**.
4. A **dip** is added to the contour at **points of glottalization** (see Figure 10-1).
5. The **final F0 value in statements is lowered by 10 Hz**.
6. The range is **narrowed** so that an excursion **above 190 Hz is rare**.

## 10.6 Potential improvements from additional syntactic information

Not implemented in MITalk (it would need a fuller parser). The original algorithm also provides for:

- identifying dependent vs. independent clauses and matrix vs. embedded clauses, for resetting declination lines and computing continuation rises;
- boundaries created by syntactic transformations: **clefting, there-insertion, preposing, topicalization, left and right dislocation, extraposition, ellipsis**;
- special contours for **appositives, vocatives, tag questions, and yes/no questions offering alternatives**.
